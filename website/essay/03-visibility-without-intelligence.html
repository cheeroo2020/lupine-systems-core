The first two chapters described a flow that is neither predictable nor measured. The obvious question is why it has stayed that way, given that the industry is large, sophisticated and well capitalised, and the answer is not that nobody noticed.

The industry noticed, built a solution, and shipped it. The solution addressed the wrong half of the problem.

3.1SWIFT does not move money

This is the single most common misunderstanding about cross-border payments, and correcting it is necessary before anything else in this chapter makes sense.

SWIFT is a messaging network. It carries instructions between financial institutions and it has never moved a dollar. The money itself moves through RTGS systems, through pre-funded nostro and vostro balances, through FX desks and through clearing houses, whereas SWIFT is what tells those systems to act.

One MT103 arriving at a correspondent triggers a sanctions screen, a treasury reservation against the nostro pool, an RTGS settlement instruction, a ledger entry and, frequently, a compliance case file. Five systems in different institutions act on one block of text, which means that if the text is ambiguous, they act differently.

3.2Three generations of the same layer

The messaging layer has been rebuilt twice, and the pattern of what each generation fixed is the argument of this chapter.

SWIFT MT103 still runs much of the world, however its fields are free text. Each hop re-keys and truncates them to fit its own systems, so a debtor name entered as LUPINE SYSTEMS PTY LTD can arrive four hops later as LUPINE SYS PTY L, with the address line dropped and the remittance reference cut short. At that point a sanctions engine cannot resolve the party, and the payment goes to manual review — which is a delay caused entirely by the format rather than by anything the payer did.

ISO 20022 replaced free text with structured XML, based on schemas that define every field, so the data survives the journey intact and arrives machine-readable and compliance-ready. This is a genuine improvement and the migration is underway across the industry.

SWIFT gpi is an overlay rather than a replacement. It bolts end-to-end tracking onto the existing rails and gives hop-level timestamps and fee transparency, which the industry had nothing comparable to beforehand.

Consider a payment that sits at the Singapore correspondent for ninety minutes waiting for liquidity. A gpi tracker will report it accurately:

Everything in that log is correct. What gpi cannot do is predict the shortage, reroute around it, or score whether a different corridor would have been faster, and it cannot do any of those things because it is an observation layer rather than a decision layer. It reports the delay at the moment the delay finishes.

gpi tracks problems. It does not prevent them. The industry solved visibility and left intelligence untouched, which is why a payer today can watch a payment be late in real time and still not be able to do anything about it.

3.4Structured data is necessary, not sufficient

ISO 20022 matters more than it is usually given credit for, however not for the reason normally cited. Banks adopt it for migration compliance and data quality, whereas the same structured fields can be read as intelligence input.

In a routing model, one payment is simultaneously a constraint set, a risk vector, a compliance object, a node on a corridor graph, a time-window object and a liquidity demand event. None of that is readable from free text, and all of it is readable from schema-validated fields.

The same property underwrites cryptographic evidence, and this is the part that carries into Chapters V and VI. To hash a field you must know exactly where it begins and ends. ISO 20022 gives discrete, labelled, validated fields; MT103 runs names, addresses and purpose codes together with no programmatic separation, so there is nothing discrete to hash.

You cannot hash ambiguity. Verifiable evidence is not merely a feature that MT103 lacks — it is structurally impossible on top of it.

3.5Why the incumbents cannot close it

The remaining question is why the firms with the most capability have not built the decision layer, and the answer is not that they are incapable. Chapter II established that integration works: Wise cut UK partner bank fees nine-fold and took Hungarian instant transfers from 17% to 82% of volume. These are firms that execute.

The constraint is structural rather than technical:

  • Wise spent a decade and sixty-three licences building its own rails across 2,500 currency routes, which is a genuine asset and also a boundary — it optimises inside its own network, whereas it cannot recommend a competitor's corridor.
  • Remitly operates 1,700 corridors with direct integrations to a hundred partners, and more than 75% of its 2020 transactions completed inside an hour. That is real engineering, applied within one network.
  • Adyen runs six live data centres with real-time rerouting on outage, which is forward correction in production since 2018 — between Adyen's own data centres, for Adyen's own traffic.
  • Banks route on treasury cost optimisation, based on pre-funded nostro accounts and correspondent agreements signed years earlier, so they ask what is cheapest for them rather than what is best for the payment.

Each of these is rational. None of them can produce a cross-provider recommendation, however, because doing so would mean routing volume away from the network that constitutes the firm's value. The neutrality that the problem requires is the one thing an incumbent cannot supply without cannibalising itself.

3.6What follows

The gap is now precisely located. Messaging is the control layer and the industry rebuilt it twice, whereas both rebuilds improved description — better structured data, better tracking — and neither added decision. The firms best placed to add it are structurally prevented from doing so by the shape of their own businesses.

Chapter IV describes what a decision layer actually requires: computing every viable path simultaneously rather than checking constraints in sequence, and producing an output that is the same every time the inputs are.
