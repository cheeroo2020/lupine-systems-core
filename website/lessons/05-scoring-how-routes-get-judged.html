Scene 02 · The function

One number from many

U(R) = α₁·Liq + α₂·Lat + α₃·FX + α₄·Rel + α₅·Comp + α₆·Exp + α₇·Urg

Every route gets a score vector, not a single number — seven separate measurements describing what the route is like.

The composite is a weighted sum of those scores. Change the weights and the same vector produces a different total.

V0 implements three of the seven: speed, cost, reliability.

Scene 03 · Normalisation

Everything to 0–1 first

Speed is measured in hours. Cost is in basis points. Reliability is already a probability. You cannot add those.

speed = 1 − time/max_time
cost  = 1 − bps/max_bps

Both become 0–1, where 1 is always best. Only then can a weight mean anything.

Three rails, raw then normalisedmax 36h · max 250 bps

rail    raw         → speed  cost Wise    1h · 45bps  → 0.97   0.82 Nium    2h · 25bps  → 0.94   0.90 SWIFT  36h · 250bps → 0.00   0.00

The slowest rail scores 0 on speed and the dearest scores 0 on cost. Normalisation is relative to the candidate set, not an absolute scale.

Scene 05 · The correction

What you said in April

Comprehension check · P2-Ch214 Apr 2026

"Speed and exposure is considered and cost and reliability is not considered."

Half right. Speed and exposure do get weighted up under pressure.

But cost and reliability are not ignored — they stay in the equation with lower weights. That difference is the whole distinction between a real optimiser and a sort-by-speed.

"We don't just pick the fastest route under pressure — we pick the best reliability-adjusted outcome under time pressure."

Scene 08 · Why a gate and not a weight

You cannot score a missing bridge

Suppose liquidity were just another weighted term at, say, 20%.

The dead corridor scores 0.00 × 0.20 = 0 on liquidity, and 0.98 and 0.99 on everything else. It could still finish top of the board and be selected — and then fail, because there is no money in the account.

A weight lets a fatal flaw be outvoted.
A gate does not.

If liquidity were weighted at 20%the failure mode

liq      0.00 × 0.20 = 0.000 latency  0.98 × 0.40 = 0.392 comp     0.99 × 0.40 = 0.396 composite          0.788 → selected

Highest score on the board. Cannot move a cent.

Scene 09 · The output

Four route tiers

Scoring does not return a winner. It returns a classification, and the winner is one of four categories.

Optimal Highest composite utility. Selected and sent to Rail.
Admissible On the Pareto frontier. Valid alternatives, not chosen this time.
Fallback Used if the optimal route fails mid-execution. Pre-computed.
Eliminated Liquidity-starved or too risky. Never selected under any weighting.

The fallback tier is what Rail uses to correct forward when a hop fails — which is Lesson 8. Scoring is where that alternate gets computed, before the money moves.

Scene 10 · The thesis

Same engine, different weights

What a naive system does

  • Sorts by one dimension at a time
  • Treats every constraint as a weight
  • Can select a corridor that cannot execute
  • Returns one answer, no alternatives
  • Same output regardless of urgency

What Aiva does

  • Normalises every score to 0–1 first
  • Eliminates the impossible before weighting
  • Shifts weights by urgency, never to zero
  • Classifies into optimal / admissible / fallback
  • Produces the fallback before it is needed
"The same scoring engine, evaluating the same corridors, produces a different best route depending on the weights. The engine doesn't change. The weights do."

Scene 11 · Check yourself

Say these out loud

Both of today's mechanisms are on your list of twelve. Neither came out right in April.

Why is liquidity a gate rather than a weight?Mechanism #6

A corridor with no liquidity physically cannot execute the payment — it does not matter how good its latency, cost or compliance scores are, because there is no money in the nostro account. As a weight, a fatal flaw can be outvoted by strong scores elsewhere and the dead corridor gets selected. Aiva eliminates impossible paths first, so no computation is spent scoring routes that can never work.

At critical urgency, what happens to cost and reliability?Mechanism #7

They stay in the equation with lower weights — cost at 5%, reliability at 35%. They are not ignored. A fast route with a 40% failure probability still loses, because reliability drags the composite down far enough. Weights shift; they never zero out.

What are the four route tiers, and which matters for Rail?

Optimal (selected), admissible (on the frontier, valid alternatives), fallback (used if the optimal fails), eliminated (never selected). Fallback is the one Rail depends on — the pre-computed alternate that lets execution correct forward instead of rolling back.
