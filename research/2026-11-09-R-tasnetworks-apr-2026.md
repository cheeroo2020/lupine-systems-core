## Summary

The report is TasNetworks' 10-year look ahead, to 2036, for Tasmania's transmission and distribution
networks. It sets out where the network is limited, what TasNetworks plans to build or replace, and where
new load or generation could connect (p. 2). Its two big themes are new **AI-factory load** in the north,
and keeping the island grid **secure** as more inverter-based generation connects. Under the rules it must
be published by 31 October each year (p. 10).

## What the source says

### The organisation and its roles
- TasNetworks is owned by the State of Tasmania, with $3.5 billion of assets and more than 300,000 customers (p. 11).
- Its roles: transmission and distribution network service provider, Jurisdictional Planning Body,
  **System Strength Service Provider**, **Inertia Service Provider** and Coordinating Network Service Provider (p. 2).

### The network
- **Transmission** (p. 35):
  - 49 substations and 8 switching stations;
  - 3,311 circuit km of line;
  - a 220 kV backbone with 110 kV around it.
- **Distribution** (p. 39):
  - 15,415 km of overhead high-voltage line;
  - 30,789 overhead distribution substations;
  - 231,643 poles.
- **Generation on the island** is about 3,400 MW (p. 2). Hydro is 2,125 MW, 62.9% of energy; wind is 568 MW, 18.2% of energy (p. 29).
- **Basslink** (HVDC, George Town to Loy Yang) became a regulated, prescribed transmission service on 1 July 2026 (p. 18).
- **Marinus Link** is 1,500 MW, built in two 750 MW stages (p. 17). It integrates in early 2030 (p. 79).

### Demand
- **2025 Tasmanian customer demand:** from 728 MW to a maximum of 1,645 MW, peaking in winter (p. 26).
- **Energy:** about 10,401 GWh in 2025, down 5%. The fall is attributed to the closure of the Liberty Bell Bay smelter (pp. 26, 30).
- **Forecasts** follow AEMO's 2025 Electricity Statement of Opportunities, planned to the 50% probability-of-exceedance level (pp. 30–31, 113).
- **AI factories** could raise annual energy use "by up to 30% by 2030" (p. 33). Maximum demand could reach about 2,300 MW by 2036 (p. 34).

### New large loads (AI factories)
- **Firmus Project Southgate, stage 1** (p. 4):
  - 104 MW contracted at St Leonards;
  - a further 288 MW at Bell Bay;
  - 52 MW at Wesley Vale.
- TasNetworks "reasonably expects the commitment of a number of these projects over the next 24 months" (p. 32).
- Loads that can quickly cut demand after a fault mean "less requirement for new transmission infrastructure" (p. 52).
- New connection standards for large inverter-based loads such as AI factories are being developed in 2026 (p. 23).

### George Town: one example worth knowing well
- **The trigger:** 300 MW or more of new load at George Town could at times be at least 60% of the whole State's load (p. 52).
- **Why that matters:** losing one asset there, such as a transformer or the 220 kV bus, could then black out the State. The planning rules forbid that.
- **The option:** a new substation with 550 Mvar of reactive support. As a contingent project it is costed at $370.6 million (pp. 52–53).

### Projects
- **North West Transmission Developments, stage 1:** $1.317 billion, Board decision May 2026. It is 129 km of double-circuit line from Palmerston to Sheffield to Burnie (pp. 17, 22, 44).
- **Central Highlands line upgrades**, due by June 2027. For example, Liapootah–Waddamana rises from 376 to 501 MVA (p. 46).
- **Renewable Energy Zone expansion**, two options:
  - Burnie–Hampshire Hills, $245 million;
  - Waddamana–Palmerston, $224 million (pp. 50–51).
- **Regulatory investment test thresholds:** $8 million for transmission (RIT-T) and $7 million for distribution (RIT-D) (p. 21).

### System security
- **Inertia:** the secure level is 3,800 MW.s, and a deficit of 3,620 MW.s is forecast from 2029–30 (pp. 82–83).
- **System strength:**
  - TasNetworks must procure "the entire amount", not only the shortfall. The obligation started 2 December 2025 (p. 80).
  - It currently contracts with existing synchronous condenser and generator owners, until June 2029 (p. 83).
  - George Town is called "a vulnerable location" (p. 87).
- **Constraints:**
  - In 2025, 76% of constrained intervals involved Basslink (p. 94).
  - Farrell–Sheffield carries up to 760 MW of West Coast generation over about 85 km (p. 55).

### Asset management (closest to an industrial engineering background)
- **The approach:** replacement is "based on asset condition and risk, rather than age" (p. 111). Overall risk weighs asset criticality and condition (p. 117).
- **Planned transformer replacements** include Sheffield T1 (2027, $10.7 million) and Rosebery (2028, $17.5 million) (p. 71).
- **Reliability, 2025–26:**
  - Urban and Regional Centres averaged 210.43 minutes of interruption a year, against a standard of 120 (SAIDI, p. 100).
  - The transmission line fault outage rate was 16.82%, against a target of 15.95 (p. 99).

### Things that don't add up in the report
Reading critically, a few figures disagree inside the report:
- **Share of maximum demand** for major customers: 40% (p. 7) versus "more than half" (p. 30).
- **Rooftop solar growth:** stated in two different ways (pp. 8, 20).
- **Scenario names:** differ between pages 23 and 31.

These are worth noticing, not quoting as errors in an interview.

## Our interpretation

- **The work is growing.** For an office-based applicant, the report shows three busy areas:
  - **connections** (AI factories, generators, new standards for large loads);
  - **planning** (George Town, North West Transmission Developments, Renewable Energy Zones);
  - **asset management** (condition-and-risk replacement, reliability below target in some areas).
- **Asset management is the closest fit to industrial and production engineering:** risk, condition, criticality, reliability, programmes.
- **The best questions** show that you read the trade-offs:
  - why a responsive large load can avoid building transmission;
  - why 60% of State load at one substation is a system-black risk.

## Ten things to be able to discuss in an interview

Practise each one aloud in two or three sentences, with its page.

1. **AI factories and George Town:** the Firmus loads, and why 300 MW at one substation is a system-black risk (pp. 4, 52–53).
2. **The planning rules:** limits on how much load one failure may interrupt, and the 850 MW southern limit (pp. 56, 114–115).
3. **North West Transmission Developments and Marinus Link:** what is committed ($1.317 billion) and what is still an option (pp. 17, 22, 48–51).
4. **Constraints:** why Basslink dominates constrained intervals, and the Farrell–Sheffield limit (pp. 55, 94).
5. **System strength and inertia:** TasNetworks' new obligations, the contracts to 2029 and the shortfalls after 2030 (pp. 80–87).
6. **Risk-based asset management:** condition and criticality rather than age, and the transformer replacement list (pp. 70–73, 111, 117).
7. **Reliability:** where targets were missed, and the programmes responding (pp. 98–103).
8. **Connection capacity:** spare capacity at substations, and firm versus non-firm supply (pp. 62–63, 108–109).
9. **Forecasting:** based on AEMO's forecasts, the 50% probability-of-exceedance level, a winter peak, and AI-factory sensitivity (pp. 31–34).
10. **The distribution transition:** rooftop solar, home and community batteries, voltage rise and EV capacity mapping (pp. 20–21, 121).
